# When Does Morpheme-Aware Tokenization Help?

## *A Predictive Framework Across Language Types*

**Proposed submission:** Master's Thesis, \*ACL  
**Proposed author:** Isaac Thompson  
**Last Updated**: Sep 15, 2026 9:00 AM MDT  
**Reviewed by**: Steve Richardson at Aug 25, 2026 12:00 PM MDT and Eric Ringger at Aug 26, 2026 8:30 AM MDT

**One-line contribution:** We show that whether morpheme-aware tokenization helps depends on a training-free, pre-computable property of the model's vocabulary, not on linguistic typology, enabling a tokenization recommendation for any new language before fine-tuning begins.

## Definitions[^1]

* **Polysynthetic:** languages that pack many morphemes (often a whole clause's worth of meaning) into a single word, e.g. Inuktitut, Mapudungun.  
* **Agglutinative:** morphemes concatenate with clear, mostly one-to-one boundaries, e.g. Turkish, Swahili.  
* **Fusional:** a single morpheme encodes multiple grammatical features simultaneously, e.g. Russian, Icelandic.  
* **Isolating/analytic:** minimal inflectional morphology; grammatical relations are expressed by word order and separate words, e.g. Vietnamese, Mandarin.  
* **Vocabulary density:** the fraction of a language's gold/reference morphemes already present in a pretrained model's existing subword vocabulary.

## Problem

Standard subword tokenization (BPE) splits at frequent, unmeaningful boundaries (Sennrich et al., 2016a; Gage, 1994), a problem for polysynthetic languages which pack many morphemes into each word. This matters because morphemes carry semantic content: splitting a word at the wrong boundary can sever a unit of meaning that the model would otherwise be able to share across related word forms and, potentially, across languages that use similar morphology. High fertility (many subword tokens per word) correlates with degraded representation quality on downstream tasks (Rust et al., 2021), and morphology-aware segmentation has shown mixed but sometimes substantial MT gains for polysynthetic languages specifically (Mager et al., 2022). Our core test languages are Mapudungun, Nahuatl, and Inuktitut[^2] (polysynthetic), Quechua (agglutinative/polysynthetic bridge case, see the Quechua bridge-case footnote below), Turkish, Finnish, and Swahili (agglutinative), Icelandic and Russian (fusional), and Vietnamese, Mandarin, and Yoruba (isolating controls).  
Morfessor-VC improved Mapudungun→Spanish, but the direction we actually care about is Spanish→Mapudungun. That's exactly the direction where Morfessor-VC's advantage shrinks and reverses at scale, delivering no clear advantage (Thompson et al., 2026), the reverse of Nzeyimana's (2024) target-side gains for Kinyarwanda. Whether this reflects tokenization-only vs. full morphological modeling, or is polysynthetic-specific, is open.  
**Research questions:** (1) is this a polysynthetic-specific problem, or does it hold across morphological types more broadly? (2) is the source/target asymmetry observed for Mapudungun a general property of morpheme-aware tokenization (helping more when generating into morphologically complex languages than out of them) across typologies, or specific to that language pair? No systematic study of either question exists yet.[^3]  
**Hypotheses:** (1) the benefit of morpheme-aware tokenization is driven primarily by vocabulary density (how much of a language's morphology already overlaps with the pretrained model's vocabulary) rather than by typological category as such; if true, morphology-aware methods should help agglutinative and fusional languages with low density, not just polysynthetic ones[^4]. (2) morpheme-aware tokenization's benefit is systematically larger on the target side (generating into a morphologically complex language) than the source side, across typological categories, because target-side generation must produce well-formed morphology token-by-token while source-side encoding only needs to interpret it[^5].

## Motivation

Teams building MT for a new language need to know which tokenization strategy to use; no such guidance exists today. Tons of morphologically complex languages are underserved or absent from commercial MT (Joshi et al., 2020). Some agglutinative languages have large speaker bases, such as Turkish and Swahili (Eberhard et al., 2026). Polysynthetic languages are often endangered indigenous languages, where MT could meaningfully help (Mager et al., 2023).   
116 of NLLB-200's 204 languages (57%) are both morphologically complex and low-resource[^6] (NLLB Team, 2022). This is the population a vocabulary-aligned tokenization method could plausibly help. Notably, Mapudungun, Nahuatl, and Inuktitut (this project's core polysynthetic languages) are not in NLLB-200 at all (verified against the official FLORES-200 language list, NLLB Team, 2022). The contribution isn't improving NLLB's existing coverage; it's extending vocabulary-aligned tokenization to languages NLLB was never trained on. The framework has a second, complementary audience: NLLB-covered morphologically complex languages in our test set, both the 116 low-resource ones (e.g., Quechua) and complex high-resource languages (e.g., Turkish, Finnish, Swahili), where better tokenization can still improve translation quality even without extending coverage to new languages.

## Prior Work & Gap

* Virpioja et al. (2013) is a tooling/implementation contribution rather than a hypothesis test. It extends the unsupervised Morfessor algorithm (Creutz & Lagus, 2002\) into a practical Python implementation with semi-supervised extensions, without evaluating a specific held research question. It doesn't account for pretrained model vocabulary, which is the gap this proposal addresses.  
* Minixhofer et al. (2022) tested whether cross-lingual static-embedding similarity could effectively initialize subword embeddings for transferring a pretrained monolingual model to a new language (WECHSEL); it did, matching or beating other transfer methods and from-scratch training at up to 64× less compute, tested on transferring English RoBERTa/GPT-2 to French, German, Chinese, and Swahili. The method adapts vocabulary to a language's script and frequency statistics, though, not to its morphology.  
* Kudo (2018) tested whether segmentation ambiguity could be harnessed as noise to improve NMT robustness (subword regularization), introducing a unigram-LM segmentation algorithm to enable it; the method showed consistent improvements, especially in low-resource and out-of-domain settings. Like standard BPE, though, the unigram objective optimizes for likelihood under a frequency-based model, not morpheme boundaries, so it inherits the same structural limitation.  
* Mager et al. (2022) tested the hypothesis that unsupervised morphological segmentation outperforms BPE for polysynthetic languages; they found it does for Nahuatl and two other polysynthetic languages, but tested only vanilla Morfessor (not vocabulary-constrained) and did not compare across typologies, which is the same single-language-or-few-languages limitation this proposal addresses.  
* Nzeyimana (2024) tested whether an explicit morphological analyzer/synthesizer, replacing tokenization entirely, outperforms subword methods; it did, beating NLLB-200 and, on the TICO-19 benchmark specifically, Google Translate for Kinyarwanda↔English, but this requires per-language analyzer tooling unavailable for Mapudungun, Nahuatl, or Inuktitut, motivating our lighter-weight, analyzer-free approach.   
* Asgari et al. (2025) tested whether constraining BPE merges to respect morpheme boundaries improves LLM training efficiency across morphologically diverse languages (MorphBPE); it did for the languages tested, but this hasn't been validated for MT quality or for genuinely low-resource languages, and (like Nzeyimana's approach) it requires per-language morphological segmentation. MorphBPE and Morfessor-VC represent opposite resolutions of the same tension: MorphBPE constrains BPE merges to fit morpheme boundaries, while Morfessor-VC constrains morpheme segmentation to fit the model's existing BPE vocabulary. Included here as a stronger-tooling comparison point against our analyzer-free methods.  
* Ebrahimi et al. (2023) report AmericasNLP shared-task results fine-tuning multilingual models on polysynthetic indigenous languages, establishing precedent for the fine-tuning-based approach this proposal builds on.  
* Thompson (2026) finds that translation direction, not morphological type, is the dominant predictor of MT metric reliability across ten metrics, and explicitly leaves open "whether the direction×morphology cell structure holds for polysynthetic languages (e.g. Inuktitut)." This proposal directly answers that open question, extending the direction-sensitivity finding from metric reliability to tokenization benefit itself.  
* Thompson et al. (2026) tested whether constraining Morfessor output to a pretrained model's existing vocabulary (Morfessor-VC) improves translation without expanding the embedding matrix; it partially validated for Mapudungun→Spanish, but the advantage shrank and reversed at scale in the reverse direction (Spanish→Mapudungun), which is the asymmetry motivating this proposal. This project extends Morfessor-VC beyond its single-language validation to test whether it generalizes across morphological types, and introduces two new methods to address the source/target asymmetry.   
* **Gap:** All prior work applies one method to one or a few languages. No comparison across typologies exists, and no framework tells when to use a strategy for a new language.

## Proposed Approach & Experimental Plan

**Phase 0 (pilot):** Test Hypothesis 1 (density as primary driver) on Mapudungun alone first[^7], where the existing data pipeline, eval harness, and Morfessor-VC baseline from the AmericasNLP paper make it the fastest path to a trustworthy first result, specifically, whether vocabulary density predicts Morfessor-VC's benefit even within a single language's train/test splits, before committing compute to the full grid.  
**Methods[^8]** (each testing a distinct hypothesis about how to reconcile morpheme boundaries with a fixed pretrained vocabulary):

* **Morfessor-VC (Vocabulary Constrained):** Hypothesis: constraining segmentation to the existing vocabulary avoids the OOV-embedding problem, but may over-restrict morpheme boundaries where vocabulary overlap is low. Beyond the chrF++ number, this tests whether Section 1's semantic-integrity claim holds even under a hard vocabulary constraint. If forcing morphemes into an ill-fitting existing vocabulary still helps, that's evidence the presence of some morpheme-aware structure matters more than getting every boundary exactly right; if it doesn't, that suggests boundary precision itself is what carries the semantic benefit, favoring methods like Morfessor-VE that don't compromise on segmentation to fit existing vocabulary. Constrains Morfessor output to the pretrained model's existing vocabulary.  
* **Vocab-biased BPE:** Hypothesis: a softer bias (upweighting rather than hard-constraining) preserves more of BPE's frequency-driven efficiency while still favoring morphologically meaningful merges. Beyond the chrF++ number, this tests whether morpheme-aware benefit is a matter of degree rather than kind. If a soft bias captures most of Morfessor-VC's gain, that suggests the semantic benefit from Section 1 comes from nudging segmentation toward morpheme boundaries, not from strictly enforcing them; if it captures little of the gain, that argues morpheme integrity is closer to a threshold effect, where partial adherence to boundaries doesn't preserve the shared semantic unit. Upweights BPE merges that produce in-vocab tokens.  
* **Morfessor-VE (Vocabulary Expanded):** Hypothesis: allowing new tokens for out-of-vocabulary morphemes, rather than forcing them into existing vocabulary, better preserves morpheme integrity at the cost of added embedding parameters. Beyond the chrF++ number, this is the direct counterpoint to Morfessor-VC's constraint: if VE outperforms VC, that's evidence boundary precision matters more than fitting the existing vocabulary, meaning the model can absorb new embeddings faster than it can recover from a severed morpheme; if VE doesn't outperform VC despite its embedding-parameter cost, that argues the model's existing vocabulary structure already captures most of what matters, and expansion is solving a problem that wasn't limiting performance. Adds OOV morphemes as new tokens, with average-embedding initialization.

**Scalable density estimation:** To extend the framework beyond the 12 test languages to the full 116 complex/low-resource NLLB-200 population (Section 2), vocabulary density is additionally estimated via an unsupervised method (overlap between an unsupervised morphological segmenter's (Morfessor) output and the pretrained tokenizer's vocabulary) requiring no gold morphological annotation. This estimate is validated against gold-derived density on the subset of languages where gold segmentation exists (Turkish, Finnish, Icelandic, Russian, possibly Quechua), and the predictive framework is reported using both gold and estimated density to quantify what is lost by removing the gold-annotation requirement.  
**Baselines:** Standard BPE, plain Morfessor, WECHSEL[^9], character-level, and MorphBPE.  
**Languages:** Mapudungun, Nahuatl, Inuktitut, Quechua[^10] (polysynthetic/agglutinative); Turkish, Finnish, Swahili (agglutinative); Icelandic, Russian (fusional); Vietnamese, Mandarin, Yoruba (analytic control).  
**Model architectures:** NLLB-200 (primary) and mBART-50. mT5 and Aya Expanse 8B are Tier 3 stretch architecture checks if time allows.  
**Evaluation:** chrF++, BLEU, and, per direction/morphology cell, CometKiwi-23 or MetricX-24 as recommended by Thompson (2026)'s typological metric-selection guide, rather than a single fixed COMET checkpoint across all language/direction pairs. For the six languages overlapping with AmanaMT's own benchmark (Turkish, Finnish, Swahili, Icelandic, Russian, Mandarin), per-language empirically measured metric reliability from that study is used directly rather than the general typological guide. Character-only chrF is additionally reported alongside chrF++ to check whether results are driven by chrF++'s word-n-gram component in typologically uneven ways.  
**Scope tiers:** Given thesis time constraints, work is tiered:

* Tier 1 (required): Phase 0 pilot; full grid on Morfessor-VC, Morfessor-VE, and standard BPE/Morfessor baselines across all 12 languages, NLLB-200 only; density-vs-typology test; minimal human eval (one language per typological category, by annotator availability, see Core plan). Tier 1 alone constitutes a complete, defensible thesis.  
* Tier 2 (if time permits): mBART-50 replication; vocab-biased BPE; gold-standard segmentation comparison; LLM relevance pilot.  
* Tier 3 (stretch): mT5 and Aya Expanse 8B architecture checks; Optuna-tuned BPE and UnigramLM as additional baselines; an exploratory PathSay-language addition (one or a few, lab data-gathering project); a free BOUQuET benchmark eval on existing Mapudungun models (inference-only, no new training); full leave-one-language/family-out toolkit validation; extensive human eval (broader language coverage, multiple annotators, measured inter-annotator agreement); resource-level stratification beyond the Finnish/Russian check; mechanistic interpretability probe: testing whether embeddings for morphologically related word forms (same root, different inflection) become more similar under morpheme-aware tokenization specifically for low-density morphemes, as a check on why density predicts benefit rather than just that it does.

**Core plan:**

* Phase 0 pilot on Mapudungun, then the full grid across all methods/baselines/languages  
* Tokenization × resource-level stratification, using Finnish and Russian (high-resource, morphologically complex) as a check that observed effects reflect morphology rather than resource level alone. For the lowest-resource languages' comparisons, high-resource languages are additionally subsampled to match sentence count, with natural-size and matched-size results reported side by side, to directly rule out corpus size as the driver.  
* Gold-standard segmentation comparison (Tier 2, exploratory): limited to languages with available gold segmentation (likely Turkish, Finnish, Icelandic, Russian, possibly Quechua via UniMorph). Treated as secondary, directional evidence only; not required for the core density-vs-typology conclusions, given sparse gold data for the core polysynthetic languages.  
* Density-vs-typology test: mixed-effects model with vocabulary density as fixed effect and language as random intercept, fit on within-language density-benefit pairs (computed per frequency bucket of morphemes) rather than a single between-language data point per language, to avoid conflating typology with per-language idiosyncrasy under n≈12. Report pooled slope and per-typology-bucket slopes to surface any residual typological confound. Includes raw pretraining-corpus token count (per language, from NLLB-200/mBART training data) as a covariate, to test whether density predicts benefit independent of raw pretraining exposure. Final tokenizer vocabulary size per method/language is also logged and reported as a covariate, to check that comparisons don't just track vocabulary size differences between Morfessor- and BPE-based methods.  
* Predictive framework (vocab density → recommended method) and toolkit release, validated via leave-one-language-out method-recommendation accuracy,[^11] with Quechua and/or Swahili held out entirely as a cold test on a language genus absent from model fitting (Quechuan; Bantu specifically. Yoruba remains in training as a distantly related but distinct Niger-Congo branch, so the Swahili holdout is a genus-level, not macro-family-level, test)  
* LLM relevance pilot (Tier 2): on 2-3 languages spanning typological categories, test whether the same density-benefit pattern holds for few-shot LLM performance (not just fine-tuned MT), using a small open-weight multilingual LLM. This is a contained pilot, not a full replication of the MT grid, intended to establish whether the framework's relevance extends beyond MT to general-purpose LLM tokenization choices. Token fertility results (Evaluation) are also reported as an inference-cost metric (tokens per query, relative to English) to quantify the equity/cost stakes of tokenization choice independent of translation quality.  
* Backtranslation (Sennrich et al., 2016b) as a scoped ablation on the polysynthetic languages only (not part of the main cross-typology comparison)  
* Minimal human eval (Tier 1): one language per typological category (polysynthetic, agglutinative, fusional, analytic), not one language per typology weighted arbitrarily, chosen by confirmed annotator availability rather than language identity, to avoid giving any single language disproportionate attention. Mapudungun fills the polysynthetic slot, with two annotators (the existing AmericasNLP annotator plus Rogers, the Mapudungun linguist collaborating on the mapudungun-syntax gold-standard project), enabling a real inter-annotator agreement measurement for this category rather than the single-annotator design the Mapudungun paper's own pilot was limited to; the other three slots still need an actual annotator audit before committing (Turkish/Finnish plausible for agglutinative, Icelandic/Russian for fusional, Vietnamese/Mandarin/Yoruba for analytic). Small fixed sample size (\~30-50 sentences per language), preference ranking between Standard BPE and best new method, following the Mapudungun paper's own pilot design. If the analytic-category slot is evaluated in the en→X direction (already tested for Vietnamese/Mandarin/Yoruba), the resulting judgments also contribute a real data point toward AmanaMT (Thompson, 2026)'s documented empty en→X isolating cell, a free secondary benefit of already-planned work, not added scope.

## Validation & Impact

**Validation:**

* Morfessor-VE is tested on both source and target sides, directly addressing the source/target asymmetry from Section 1\.  
* Analytic languages serve as a control / falsifiability check, due to minimal morphology.  
* Architecture replication tests whether results are architecture-specific or general.  
* Tokenization × resource-level stratification: whether benefit is only at low resource.  
* Gold-standard segmentation comparison tests whether the methods are linguistically meaningful, not just MT-useful.  
* Minimal human eval (one language per typological category) tests whether the automatic-metric "help" threshold (defined in an above footnote) reflects perceptible quality differences beyond the single data point already available from the Mapudungun paper's own pilot.  
* Leave-one-language-out validation reports recommendation accuracy and calibration against pre-specified thresholds.[^12]  
* The density-vs-typology test directly addresses whether typological category or vocabulary overlap is the true mechanism behind tokenization benefit, a claim broader than the polysynthetic-language motivation in Section 1\.  
* The held-out-family test (Section 4\) evaluates whether the framework predicts correctly for a language whose family was never used in fitting, not just a language absent from training within an already-seen family.  
* Density-exposure disentanglement: as a causal check, vocabulary density is artificially reduced for a subset of a high-resource language (e.g., Turkish) by removing selected subword merges, holding pretraining exposure constant. If the density-benefit relationship replicates within-language under this manipulation, that is stronger evidence for density as a mechanism rather than a proxy for pretraining exposure.  
* The source/target asymmetry test (Hypothesis 2\) directly evaluates whether the Mapudungun-specific finding in Section 1 generalizes across typologies, distinguishing a language-pair idiosyncrasy from a general property of morpheme-aware tokenization.  
* The gold-vs-estimated density comparison validates that the framework's core predictive claim survives removal of the gold-annotation requirement, supporting the scale claim in Section 2 (116/204 NLLB languages).

**Impact:** If vocab density predicts the winning method, the framework gives practitioners a principled choice without needing to train first, which directly serves the "which tokenization should I use for a new language" need in Section 2\. The token-fertility results additionally speak to inference cost and equity of access for morphologically complex languages, broadening relevance beyond translation quality alone. Because density can be estimated without gold morphological annotation, the framework is deployable across the full population of complex/low-resource languages described in Section 2, not only the 12 languages tested here. A mechanistic account of this relationship, for instance, whether morpheme-aware tokenization measurably tightens embedding similarity across inflected forms of the same root for low-density morphemes, would strengthen the causal story beyond the correlational density-benefit relationship, and is a natural stretch goal if time permits.

[^1]:  Typological categories following Comrie, 1989 and Velupillai, 2012; see also WALS Chapter 20A, Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013\. These categories describe two related but distinct axes, boundary clarity (agglutinative vs. fusional) and degree of synthesis (polysynthetic vs. not), so a language can in principle carry more than one label (see the Quechua bridge-case footnote below).

[^2]:  Nunavut Hansard spans 1999 to 2017\. Per Thompson (2026)'s documented Simpson's-Paradox pattern from pooling MT evaluation across heterogeneous system-generation years, Inuktitut results should be checked for year-stratification sensitivity before being pooled into a single aggregate.

[^3]:  Operational definition: a method "helps" for a given language if it yields a statistically significant (paired bootstrap, p \< 0.05) improvement of ≥1 chrF++ point over the strongest baseline (standard BPE or plain Morfessor, whichever scores higher) on held-out test data. A power analysis using Phase 0 effect-size variance will be run before the full grid to check whether p\<0.05 is achievable given expected test-set sizes for the lowest-resource languages (Mapudungun, Nahuatl, Inuktitut); if underpowered, results for these languages will additionally be reported via bootstrap credible intervals alongside the significance test, disclosed as a limitation. The ≥1 chrF++ point threshold for "help" is a lower bar than what Thompson et al. (2026) found perceptible. A 3-point chrF++ gap between Standard BPE and Morfessor-VC produced no consistent human preference (24 to 30% tie rate) in that paper's pilot human evaluation. Results below roughly this margin should be read as statistically detected, not necessarily human-perceptible, gains. Multiple comparisons across languages are corrected via Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control, scoped within-language (across the candidate methods compared for that language) rather than globally across the full grid, given the power constraints already noted above.

[^4]:  This parallels a structurally similar negative result in Thompson (2026): a WALS-feature-based predictor of MT metric choice achieved poor recommendation accuracy, leading to the conclusion that "typological features alone will not pick the metric for you." Hypothesis 1 tests whether the same pattern holds for tokenization method choice, that a concrete, model-specific signal (vocabulary density) outpredicts abstract typological category, as it did there for metric selection. Note this is a distinct construct from "tokenization density" in other unpublished work (a measure of how a language's script/tokens are represented for cross-lingual retrieval transfer, not morpheme-vocabulary overlap for tokenization-method choice) — the two shouldn't be conflated despite the similar name; that work found density-like measures don't predict retrieval-transfer variation, which says nothing about whether vocabulary density predicts tokenization-method benefit here, a different mechanism entirely.

[^5]:  This hypothesis is entangled with a separate, previously documented confound. Translation direction predicts not just translation difficulty but metric reliability itself (Thompson, 2026), so an apparent lack of target-side benefit could partly reflect COMET/chrF++ being less reliable when evaluating into a given language rather than a genuine null effect. Where possible, results are cross-checked against the direction-appropriate metric per Thompson (2026)'s typological metric-selection guide (Section 4).

[^6]:  Morphological complexity classified via WALS (Dryer & Haspelmath, 2013\) Chapter 20A directly for 39 of 204 NLLB languages (a reproducible lookup-table classification); the remaining 165 classified by genus/family-level typological judgment informed by the general typology literature (Comrie, 1989; Velupillai, 2012), not by a per-language table in either source. Languages coded agglutinative, fusional, or introflexive (collectively "complex," i.e. non-isolating) versus isolating; no language in this set classified polysynthetic. Resource level per NLLB's \<1M-bitext threshold (NLLB Team, 2022). Denominator is all 204 NLLB languages; 116/204 met both criteria.

[^7]:  The AVENUE corpus is not evenly pooled across dialects: by transcription volume, approximately 97% comes from a single source community, with two much smaller supplementary sources making up the remainder. Phase 0 results (and any Mapudungun result throughout) should be read as reflecting predominantly one dialect/speaker community's usage, not a balanced cross-dialect sample.

[^8]:  Hyperparameters for all three methods are pre-registered from a small fixed grid (3-5 configs each), selected using only the Phase 0 Mapudungun dev set, then frozen identically across all languages in the full grid, which avoids per-language tuning bias and bounding engineering cost.

[^9]:  WECHSEL tests representation/embedding transfer, not vocabulary alignment. A WECHSEL win or loss is not directly interpreted as evidence for or against the tokenization hypothesis.

[^10]:  Quechua is agglutinative in morpheme-boundary clarity but exhibits polysynthetic-level morphological synthesis (high morphemes-per-word); we retain both labels and treat it as a bridge case between the two core groups (Comrie, 1989; Velupillai, 2012). Fit on all languages but one, predict the held-out language's best method, report recommendation accuracy and calibration (see the LOO accuracy/calibration footnote below).

[^12]:  (a) Top-1 recommendation accuracy against a random baseline of 1/4 (four candidate methods: the three proposed methods plus standard BPE, chance \= 25%); (b) calibration: correlation between predicted and observed chrF++ gain on the held-out language, with r \> 0.5 pre-specified as the threshold for "meaningfully predictive."
